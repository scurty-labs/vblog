Pictures make a blog feel inhabited, but most pictures on the internet come with strings attached: attribution requirements, non-commercial clauses, or a stock licence that quietly forbids the thing you were about to do. The way around it is to use work that has been given away on purpose.

## Two kinds of "free to use"

**Public domain** work has no copyright left, either because it expired or because the author never had one. A painting from 1889, a photograph taken by a government employee as part of their job, a work whose author died more than seventy years ago — all public domain in most jurisdictions.

**CC0** is a deliberate dedication. The creator keeps the copyright but waives every right they can waive, so the work can be used, changed and sold without credit. It is the closest thing to public domain that a living author can sign.

Neither kind requires attribution. Giving credit anyway is polite, and it is a useful habit: it records where a picture came from, so the next person can check.

## Where to look

- **Wikimedia Commons** hosts tens of millions of files and states the licence of each one plainly. Filter for public domain or CC0 before you search.
- **NASA and other space agencies** publish most of their imagery into the public domain; a credit line is requested, not required.
- **Museums with open access programmes** release high-resolution photographs of public-domain art without restrictions.
- **CC0 photo collections** exist for the everyday pictures — desks, coffee, streets — that stock sites charge for.

The one trap is a site that says "free" and means "free with an account and our licence". Read the licence, not the badge.

## Using them here

Upload a picture on `/media`, give it a title and alt text, then copy its link into a post. Two or more images in the same paragraph become a gallery automatically, which is how the grid above was made. Files live in `media/` on disk, so a backup is a folder copy rather than a database export.
